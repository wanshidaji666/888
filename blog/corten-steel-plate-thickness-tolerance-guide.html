Corten Steel Plate Thickness & Tolerance: A Buyer's Guide

Specification · 6 min read · Shandong Dazhi Technical Team

Every hot-rolled plate varies in thickness. Rolling mills work to allowed bands — tolerances — defined by the delivery standard, and a plate that is legitimately "on tolerance" can still be thinner than the number on your order. For weathering steel buyers this matters more than most: structural calculations, facade flatness and even how the patina reads visually all depend on actual thickness. This guide explains the ranges we roll, the tolerance standards that govern them, and the four lines buyers should add to every purchase order.

Common thickness ranges

  • Plate: 1.5–100 mm in A588, A242 and EN 10025-5 grades, in standard mill widths up to ~2,500 mm and lengths up to 12 m; cut-to-size parts can be supplied from any parent plate.
  • Coil and sheet: 1.5–12.7 mm hot-rolled, in widths up to 2,350 mm or slit to your strip width. Thin architectural panels are typically ordered at 2–4 mm; roofing and cladding profiles commonly run 1.5–3 mm.

Below roughly 2 mm, weathering steel is usually supplied as cold-reduced sheet with a different tolerance regime — confirm the product form before specifying.

What "tolerance" actually means

Tolerance is the permitted deviation of actual thickness from nominal, measured at specified points away from the plate edges. It is not a quality defect — it is a manufacturing reality written into the standard:

  • ASTM A6 / A6M governs thickness tolerance for ASTM plate (A588, A242). It permits a minus deviation of 0.3 mm on most thicknesses, with a plus deviation that grows with thickness.
  • EN 10029 governs EN plate (S355J0WP/J2W). It defines three tolerance classes — A, B and C — which differ in how the deviation is split between minus and plus. Class B or C are the usual choices for structural work with tight minus limits.
  • ASTM A568 / EN 10051 govern hot-rolled coil, sheet and strip. Tolerances here are tighter than for plate but vary with width and thickness; slit strip is measured at the coil edges where thickness runs thinnest.

Why undersize matters

An allowed minus tolerance is still real metal missing. The consequences depend on the application:

  • Structural calculations: engineers design to nominal thickness with code-specified allowances. A 10 mm plate delivered at 9.6 mm may be legal under Class A but will not suit a design computed on 10 mm minimum — or will force a redesign review.
  • Facade flatness and oil-canning: thin cladding sheet at the bottom of the tolerance band is more prone to visible distortion, especially on large, flat panels.
  • Machined parts: if the fabricator machines or grinds the face, undersize stock can drop the finished part below drawing thickness.
  • Weight and payment: plate priced by theory weight uses nominal thickness; actual delivered weight follows real thickness. A consistent minus-side supply effectively delivers less steel than the invoice implies.
Buyer's tip: If your design is thickness-critical, write "EN 10029 Class C" or "no minus tolerance" on the order — and expect a small premium, since the mill must roll heavier to guarantee it. It is far cheaper than rejecting steel after inspection.

How to order thickness correctly

  1. State the tolerance standard and class: e.g. "6 mm EN 10029 Class B" or "3/4 in. ASTM A6". Ambiguity defaults to the loosest interpretation.
  2. Specify flatness where it matters: EN 10029 flatness classes (and ASTM A6 flatness) control deviation of the plate surface. Facade and laser-cutting work benefits from the stricter classes; the mill needs this on the order, not after rolling.
  3. Declare the payment basis: theory weight (calculated from nominal thickness) or actual weighed weight. For Class C / no-undersize orders the difference can exceed 3%, so agree it before the contract.
  4. Confirm measurement points: for strip and sheet, agree where thickness is measured — mid-width versus edge — because coil edges run thinner.
