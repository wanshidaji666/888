Alloy 625 vs Monel 400 vs Incoloy 825 vs C-276: How to Choose

A buyer's guide to matching your service environment to the right nickel alloy plate — without over-specifying.

Buyer's Guide · 7 min read · ALLOYS Technical Team

Most buyers do not choose the wrong grade because they lack data — they choose the wrong grade because four alloys with similar-sounding names solve completely different problems. This guide maps the four workhorse nickel alloy plate grades to the environments they were designed for, explains the composition logic behind each family, and shows where the money goes.

Start with the environment, not the alloy

The fastest route to the right grade is to describe the media, temperature and pressure first, then let the chemistry follow. The mapping below resolves roughly 80% of the inquiries we handle:

  • Seawater, hydrofluoric acid, reducing media → Monel 400 (UNS N04400). The nickel-copper pair is close to immune in flowing seawater and stands up to HF at all concentrations up to boiling — a combination no stainless steel can touch.
  • Sulfuric and phosphoric acid, flue gas desulfurization (FGD), acid pickling → Incoloy 825 (UNS N08825). Its Ni-Fe-Cr balance with molybdenum, copper and titanium handles intermediate-aggression acids at a mid-range price.
  • Wet chlorine gas, hypochlorite, chloride-bearing oxidizing acids and the most aggressive mixed chemicals → Hastelloy C-276 (UNS N10276). When the process chart says "nothing else survives," C-276 is the default answer.
  • High-temperature strength plus corrosion, aerospace and gas turbine service → Alloy 625 (UNS N06625). The niobium-strengthened Ni-Cr-Mo chemistry keeps strength at 650 °C and above while resisting oxidation and pitting.
Tip: When in doubt, send us the media, concentration, temperature and whether the service is reducing or oxidizing. Nine times out of ten the correct grade becomes obvious in minutes — and it is occasionally a cheaper one than you expected.

Composition logic: three families, not four random alloys

Understanding the families makes the selection rational instead of memorized:

Ni-Cu family (Monel 400)

Copper gives excellent resistance in reducing, non-oxidizing media — HF, seawater, neutral salts — but the family has no chromium, so it cannot fight oxidizing acids or high-temperature oxidation. It is also the least alloyed, and normally the least expensive, of the four.

Ni-Fe-Cr family (Incoloy 825)

Replacing part of the nickel with iron reduces cost while chromium adds oxidizing-acid resistance. Additions of molybdenum and copper broaden the acid range, and titanium stabilizes the structure against sensitization. The result is the economical general-purpose chemical plant alloy.

Ni-Cr-Mo family (Alloy 625 and C-276)

Chromium handles oxidizing media, molybdenum handles reducing media and pitting, and the high combined content covers both. Alloy 625 adds niobium for precipitation strengthening — that is where its exceptional high-temperature strength comes from. C-276 pushes molybdenum to 15–17% and keeps carbon extremely low for as-welded corrosion resistance — that is why it leads in wet chlorine and hypochlorite.

Cost ordering — and why quotes expire fast

As a rough rule, plate pricing follows nickel plus molybdenum content: Monel 400 < Incoloy 825 < Alloy 625 ≈ C-276. Because 825 substitutes iron for part of the nickel and carries only 2.5–3.5% molybdenum, it frequently delivers 80% of the performance of the Ni-Cr-Mo grades at a visibly lower price — which is exactly why it dominates FGD and acid pickling duty.

Conversely, jumping to C-276 "for safety" on a job that 825 covers can add a 40–60% premium to the material line. Our recommendation: specify for the documented service conditions, and let us flag any equivalent that saves cost.

One practical consequence of the alloying content: nickel and molybdenum are exchange-traded metals, so mill prices move with the LME. Our quotations are typically valid 3–7 days; longer projects should discuss a surcharge or fixed-price window before committing volumes.

A quick decision sequence

  1. Write down the media, concentration, temperature and pressure — plus whether the environment is reducing or oxidizing.
  2. Check for chlorides, wet chlorine or hypochlorite: if present in strength, go directly to C-276.
  3. Check for high-temperature structural duty (above ~550 °C): that points to Alloy 625.
  4. Seawater or HF without oxidizing contaminants: Monel 400.
  5. Everything else acidic at moderate aggression: Incoloy 825 is usually the value choice.

If two steps point to different grades, the more aggressive condition wins — or send us both scenarios and we will quote the options side by side.
